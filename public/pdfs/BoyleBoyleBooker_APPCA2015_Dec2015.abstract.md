# Abstract

**Objective:** To quantify hand span variations among pianists by gender and ethnicity, and evaluate whether the standard piano keyboard is ergonomically appropriate for the piano-playing population.

**Methodology:** Hand span data were collected from 473 adult pianists, 49 children/teenagers, and 216 non-pianist university students using active finger span measurements (thumb-to-fifth finger and index-to-fifth finger) for both hands. Data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics, with participants categorized by gender, ethnicity (Caucasian, Asian, Other/Mixed), and performance acclaim level.

**Key Findings:** 
- Gender differences were consistent with previous studies, showing males having significantly larger hand spans than females (10-20% difference range based on cited literature)
- Ethnic differences were statistically significant but smaller in magnitude than gender differences
- Highly acclaimed solo performers demonstrated larger hand spans than other pianists
- Hand span variation among pianists was extensive, with the largest spans exceeding the smallest by approximately 10 cm (equivalent to four piano keys)
- A majority of pianists were classified as having "small hands" based on proposed metrics
- The difference between smallest and largest hand spans in the University of North Texas dataset was 4 inches (10 cm)

**Conclusions:** The current standard piano keyboard, standardized in the 1880s based on male virtuosos like Liszt, is ergonomically too large for the majority of pianists. This size mismatch creates disadvantages for pianists with smaller hands, potentially increasing pain and injury risk while limiting repertoire accessibility. The findings support the need for ergonomically-scaled piano keyboards (ESPKs) with narrower keys to accommodate the diverse hand sizes in the piano-playing population, particularly benefiting those with smaller hands who represent the majority of players.
